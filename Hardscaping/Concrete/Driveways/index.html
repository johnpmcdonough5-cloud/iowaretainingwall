Professional concrete driveway installation in Eastern Iowa

If you're interested in having your driveway installed by professionals, you've come to the right place. We are a team of experienced concrete driveway installers serving the Eastern Iowa area and surrounding communities.

For years now, we've been helping people in the Eastern Iowa area transform their outdoor spaces with beautiful, durable concrete driveways. Whether you need a new driveway built from scratch or an existing surface replaced, we can handle the project from design through final installation.

When do you need the service?

Concrete or pavers: choosing the right driveway surface

A poured concrete driveway gives you a single continuous slab rather than individual units, which means no joints for weeds or ants to work into and no stones that can shift or settle out of place over time. It's a straightforward, durable surface for daily vehicle traffic.

Pavers cost more to install but are easier to repair in sections if one area is damaged, since an installer can lift and replace individual units without redoing the whole surface. Concrete repairs usually mean patching or saw-cutting a section of slab instead.

What goes into a driveway that holds up in Iowa

A driveway slab needs a properly compacted gravel base underneath it before any concrete is poured. Skipping or under-building that base is the most common reason a driveway settles or cracks early, regardless of how good the concrete itself is. Control joints are cut into the slab at regular intervals so that as the concrete naturally shrinks and shifts with temperature, it cracks at those joints instead of randomly across the surface.

The apron, the section connecting the driveway to the street or garage slab, takes the most direct wear from turning tires and often needs additional reinforcement since it's a transition point between two surfaces that can move independently. Drainage matters just as much as the concrete itself: a driveway needs a slight grade away from the garage and house so water doesn't pool against the foundation or sit on the slab through a freeze.

Common concrete driveway problems — and how we fix them

  1. 1. Freeze-Thaw Cracking

    Problem: Water that gets into small cracks or under the slab expands as it freezes, widening cracks with every freeze-thaw cycle through an Iowa winter.

    Solution: Seal cracks early before winter to keep water out. Larger cracks may need saw-cutting into a proper control joint, or a section replaced if the damage has spread underneath the slab.

  2. 2. Surface Scaling

    Problem: The top layer of concrete can flake or peel away, most often from de-icing salt use combined with freeze-thaw cycling before the concrete had a chance to fully cure.

    Solution: Light scaling can be resurfaced with a bonding overlay. Preventing it going forward means avoiding de-icing salts on new concrete for at least the first winter and using sand for traction instead.

  3. 3. Settling or Sinking Slabs

    Problem: A slab section can sink or tilt when the base underneath wasn't compacted properly or has eroded over time, often near the apron where drainage concentrates.

    Solution: Minor settling can sometimes be corrected with slab lifting. More significant sinking usually means removing the affected section, rebuilding the base correctly, and repouring.

  4. 4. Pooling Water at the Apron

    Problem: Without enough slope away from the garage, water collects at the low point where the driveway meets the garage slab, which accelerates cracking and creates ice in winter.

    Solution: Regrading a section or adding a channel drain at the apron redirects water before it can pool. This is best addressed at the design stage of a new driveway rather than after the fact.

  5. 5. Joint Sealant Failure

    Problem: The flexible sealant in control and expansion joints breaks down over time from UV exposure and movement, letting water and debris into the joint itself.

    Solution: Old sealant should be cut out and replaced periodically. This is a small, inexpensive step that prevents water from reaching the base beneath the slab.
